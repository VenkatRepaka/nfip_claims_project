# Classify NFIP blanks before any fill

A Jupyter notebook for the 2020–2025 OpenFEMA NFIP Redacted Claims v3 extract should profile every published column on the raw file, then sort each blank into a documented state before any value is filled. One row is one redacted claims record with its own `id`: it is not a policy term, not a payment installment, and not a claim that can be joined to a policy. The full public file listed on the dataset page holds **2,725,989** records, is updated about monthly, and lags the system of record, so every year-of-loss total, including 2025, is a snapshot as of `asOfDate`. FEMA’s v3 field table defines only a few empty values in plain language; the rest are read from TRRP reporting rules the NFIP data FAQ says still apply, from sentinels such as `elevationDifference` **9999.0** and water-depth codes **99** and **999**, or from privacy redaction. Little and Rubin’s MCAR, MAR, and MNAR labels apply only after those design blanks are removed, and the observed table can reject MCAR without deciding between MAR and MNAR. Numeric imputation is the wrong default for the high-missing fields: structural zeros and explicit unknown levels carry the information, while mean, median, mode, and multivariate fills each invent a different distortion and, if used at all, are fit on training years alone. Where the local cleaning script goes past those sources — water-depth codes turned into NaN, a state-median CRS class, county-only coordinate fills, and an age measured from **1900-01-01** — the notebook follows the definition and shows the script’s choice as a diff.

## One published row is a redacted claims record

The notebook’s first cell should state the grain in a fixed order, because the dataset page and the NFIP data FAQ use the word “transaction” for two different things. The v3 page says the file “provides details on flood insurance claims transactions,” “represents more than 2.7 million claims transactions,” is redacted for personally identifiable information, is refreshed approximately monthly, lags the system of record, and “is not intended to be an official federal report.” The same page lists CSV and Parquet record count **2,725,989** and defines `id` as the unique identifier assigned to the record ([NFIP Redacted Claims v3](https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3)). The FAQ then says the current claims data do not include a payment date or a claims transaction, and that users can total losses by date of loss and cannot report disbursements by calendar year from this dataset ([FAQs About NFIP Data](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/flood-maps-and-data/faqs-about-nfip-data)). v3 nevertheless publishes `mostRecentPaymentDate`. Both pages were live when read, and neither reconciles the sentences. The notebook prints both sentences and does not “fix” the conflict by dropping rows. The official FEMA PDF linked from the dataset page did not yield text in this research pass; FAQ citations here follow the FloodSmart page, and the older Norfolk copy of the same FAQ is used only where it is named.

`dateOfLoss` is the date water first entered the insured building, `yearOfLoss` is the calendar year of that loss, `openDate` is when the claim was opened, and `mostRecentPaymentDate` is the most recent date a payment was issued ([NFIP Redacted Claims v3](https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3)). `policyCount` is insured units in an active status, and a Residential Condominium Building Association Policy is stored as one contract that insures multiple units and therefore represents multiple policies. Policy grain lives in a different file: the policy dataset is transactional across renewals, and OpenFEMA does not publish a record identifier that joins a claim to a policy. Policy number and repetitive-loss identifier are withheld ([FAQs About NFIP Data](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/flood-maps-and-data/faqs-about-nfip-data)). The local extract is the year-of-loss slice, not the national file. Claims history on OpenFEMA runs from 1978 to present, and the FAQ’s “all claims records are preserved” sentence is about retention, not about the newest loss year already being complete. The project dictionary records a single `asOfDate`. The notebook prints its minimum, maximum, and number of distinct values in the header of the year section and treats every completeness sentence as relative to that timestamp. A snapshot dated after the end of calendar 2025 still leaves 2025 incomplete as a claims year.

Five documented facts make a loss year short, or its dollars low, after the calendar year has ended. The extract lags the system of record and is refreshed on a monthly cycle; the FAQ’s aim is a refresh every **40–60 days** ([FAQs About NFIP Data](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/flood-maps-and-data/faqs-about-nfip-data); [NFIP Redacted Claims v3](https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3)). The Norfolk copy of the FAQ says the legacy system takes several months to resolve errors and that totals vary from release to release; the live FloodSmart page is softer, saying critical errors are rejected, minor errors may remain, and abnormal values can persist into OpenFEMA ([Norfolk NFIP data FAQ](https://data.norfolk.gov/api/views/suf7-r643/files/ba36ea87-3e70-47b9-a3c0-1e78b85098b7?download=true&filename=NFIP_Data_Frequently_Asked_Questions_FAQs+%281%29.pdf)). A claim can open after the loss. Payments and `mostRecentPaymentDate` can still change, and a negative paid amount is a documented reissue when a check is not cashed, so a non-null payment is not a frozen final number ([NFIP Redacted Claims v3](https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3)). There is no open-or-closed field. Closed-without-payment is represented, when populated, by the two non-payment reason codes. A lower 2025 mean payment is therefore not a severity finding. The notebook builds one row per `yearOfLoss` from 2020 through 2025 with row count, distinct loss dates, distinct event names, the sum of non-null building and contents payments, the share of those payments that are null, the share of null payment dates, the median and upper tail of `openDate` minus `dateOfLoss` in days, the count of negative lags, and the maximum loss, open, and payment dates, with `asOfDate` in the caption. It does not rank 2025 against 2020–2023 as a finished year, and it says 2024 can still move for losses late in that year. If it also counts rows by year of `mostRecentPaymentDate`, that column is labeled a payment-date profile, null payment dates stay out of every year, and the FAQ’s endorsed annual total remains loss year. No retrieved FEMA page gives a development factor or a cutoff such as “ignore the latest N months.” The notebook quotes the lag and the non-static totals and does not invent a maturity window. It also lists calendar dates in 2020–2025 with zero claims, as a presence check. An empty date is not evidence that no flood occurred.

Summary statistics start from `DataFrame.describe` and then add what `describe` omits. For numeric columns, `describe` returns count, mean, standard deviation, minimum, the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles, and the maximum, and it skips NaN. For object columns it returns count, unique, top, and freq, and a tie for the most common value is broken arbitrarily. Default `describe` on a mixed frame returns numeric columns only ([pandas.DataFrame.describe](https://pandas.pydata.org/pandas-docs/stable/reference/api/pandas.DataFrame.describe.html)). The notebook adds, for every column, the filtered row count, non-null count, missing count, missing percent, and the number of distinct non-null values, plus a second distinct count that treats missing as its own level. Text columns count blank strings separately from nulls, because the dictionary does not say which one OpenFEMA uses. Numeric columns add the 1st, 5th, 95th, and 99th percentiles, skewness, excess kurtosis, and counts of zeros, negatives, and infinities. A sentinel block is computed on the raw values, before any replacement. A five-number summary sits beside the mean: minimum, lower quartile or Tukey lower hinge, median, upper quartile or hinge, and maximum. Sample quartiles from `describe` are not guaranteed to equal Tukey hinges ([Tukey hinges](https://sdrfoundation.org/tukey-hinges-guide)). ydata-profiling’s default skewness warning fires only above 20, its low-categorical threshold is 5 distinct values, and its cardinality warning defaults to 50; those are tool defaults, not NFIP standards, so the notebook prints the numbers and still emits a full frequency table for every published code list longer than five levels ([ydata-profiling settings](https://docs.profiling.ydata.ai/4.5/advanced_settings/available_settings/)).

Coded fields with a legal-value list get every level, with an explicit missing row and a row for observed values that are not in the list. ydata’s default of five displayed categories is too small for zone, cause, deductible, rate method, condominium type, foundation, state, non-payment reason, occupancy, CRS, and floors. High-cardinality fields — ZIP, community number, community name, census block group, event designation — get the distinct count, the missing percent, the top 15 with counts, the share of rows outside that top 15, and the number of levels that occur once. Deductible fields are codes. The notebook profiles the code and maps dollars in a derived column, where code **0 is $500**, not a zero deductible and not “no contents coverage,” and code H is $200 for group policies ([NFIP Redacted Claims v3](https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3)). `numberOfFloorsInTheInsuredBuilding` is likewise a code (one floor, two, three or more, split-level, manufactured home, townhouse), not a story count. Boolean flags get value counts for true, false, and missing; the retrieved `describe` contract does not spell out a boolean index. ydata’s boolean imbalance threshold of 0.5 will flag almost every NFIP indicator, so the notebook also marks a class that exceeds 95 percent and one that exceeds 99 percent. Those cutoffs are notebook labels. Dates get non-null count, missing percent, distinct count, minimum, maximum, and the sentinel dates named below, plus the share of rows where `yearOfLoss` disagrees with the calendar year of `dateOfLoss`. The project dictionary says they match; the notebook measures the mismatch. `openDate` minus `dateOfLoss` is a reporting-lag table: count, missing, negatives, and the median through the upper tail.

Dollar fields get the full numeric block, the sum of non-null values labeled as nominal dollars, and the count of nulls excluded from that sum. Nulls are not filled before the sum. A second block restricted to values strictly greater than zero separates the zero mass from the size of paid or damaged claims. That split is a descriptive device. The FAQ supports talking about the magnitude of losses by date of loss; it does not define the split. The notebook also reports the share of rows sitting at the observed maximum, so a cap is visible. ICC “provides up to **$30,000**” ([NFIP Redacted Claims v3](https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3)). The v3 text retrieved here does not state a building or contents coverage cap; the caps discussed later come from the coverage brochure, not from a sentence that every paid amount stops at one number. Negatives are counted on every dollar field. They are a documented payment event on the gross building and contents amounts, and they stay in the profile. One presence table, sorted by missing percent descending and then by distinct count ascending, carries dtype, missingness, distinct count, the share of the top value or of true, the zero share where numeric, the sentinel count, and a blank stamp the author fills later with keep or drop. The project dictionary’s rates are an inventory. The notebook recomputes every one of them.

Duplicate checks count three different things and keep the labels distinct. Exact-row duplicates use `duplicated(keep=False)` for the number of rows that sit in an all-column duplicate group and `duplicated()` for the number of extra copies after keeping one, including `id` ([pandas.DataFrame.duplicated](https://pandas.pydata.org/pandas-docs/stable/reference/api/pandas.DataFrame.duplicated.html)). The same two counts are repeated after dropping `id`, and again after dropping a constant `asOfDate`, so a snapshot column cannot hide a repeated extract. Duplicate `id` values are a load failure relative to the published definition of a unique record key, not a second transaction to model. There is no public claim number. A repeated `(dateOfLoss, state, reportedZipCode)`, census id, or rounded coordinate is not a duplicate claim: privacy rounding, RCBAP `policyCount` greater than 1, and distinct buildings in the same block group all produce repeated keys. The notebook reports the distinct count of the key, the share of rows whose key occurs more than once, and the maximum multiplicity, under the label “repeated keys under redaction.” A finer key that also includes occupancy, the two coverage limits, and construction date is a cardinality diagnostic. Rows that share it stay in the file. Whether `duplicated` treats NaN as equal to NaN has to be confirmed on the installed pandas before sparse columns are interpreted. No retrieved OpenFEMA page says whether two rows can be successive versions of one claim, or whether `id` is stable across monthly refreshes.

Privacy redaction is part of what the file contains. Name, policy number, repetitive-loss id, street address, ZIP+4, census block, full coordinates, and the WYO company, agency, agent, and adjusting firm are withheld. Latitude and longitude that are present are truncated to **one decimal place**, described as accurate to within **about six miles**, and can fall in the wrong county or state. Mapped points look clustered because of that anonymization. The coordinate fields say to aggregate with state and county; the FAQ says census tract and county, derived from the geocode, are the best aggregation fields. The notebook states both instructions. FEMA does not recommend the file as input to a commercial catastrophe model, because truncated coordinates shift location and can throw off elevation, and it says to use the provided elevation data as a descriptive field for aggregation only ([FAQs About NFIP Data](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/flood-maps-and-data/faqs-about-nfip-data); [NFIP Redacted Claims v3](https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3)). `censusGeoid` is a block-group id, coarser than the withheld census block. `countyCode` is illustrated as `011` for Broward County, so it is the county portion and repeats across states, and the field “may not reflect the individual county” because a project can span counties. `reportedCity` carries the dictionary note that the city value is currently unavailable. The literal string in the extract is a local measurement; the notebook prints the distinct values, expects a single unavailable level, and does not geocode it. More recent geography is more reliable than older geography. FEMA has already omitted some low-confidence fields and does not name them, which is why an empty column that is still on the schema is profiled rather than assumed to be an official drop.

## Water-depth 99 is a measurement, not a hole

The v3 dictionary, the TRRP manuals, and the local cleaning script do not describe the same empty cell. The notebook keeps those layers apart. A blank is eligible for a missing-data label only when a real value could have been recorded and was not. Structural inapplicability, a meaningful zero, a sentinel that encodes a real state, a retired field, and privacy suppression are design. The FAQ’s general rule is that the public file is what WYO companies reported, that business rules reject critical errors and can allow minor ones, that “**99XX**” is often a placeholder when a required value was not reported, and that low-confidence fields were omitted rather than repaired ([FAQs About NFIP Data](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/flood-maps-and-data/faqs-about-nfip-data); [OpenFEMA FAQ](https://www.fema.gov/about/openfema/faq)). DHS OIG found that the legacy NFIP system allowed duplicate records, missing data, and invalid entries, and that in October 2019 the vast majority of that historical data, including errors, was migrated into PIVOT ([OIG-21-04](https://www.oig.dhs.gov/sites/default/files/assets/2020-11/OIG-21-04-Nov20.pdf)). That is data-quality background. It is not a second official sentinel list.

The research notes disagree on water depth, and the note that opened the reporting manuals wins. The v3 sentence defines `waterDepth` as depth in inches and warns that some measurements were provided in feet; it names no code 99. Exterior and interior depth are also “in inches,” and the v3 wording ties both to the lowest adjacent grade, again with no sentinel ([NFIP Redacted Claims v3](https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3)). The 2015 TRRP plan, which the FAQ says still governs historical practice, defines the older relative water depth as a signed integer and says that when only an appurtenant building is damaged and water has not reached the main building, the depth “is coded as **+99**.” Its narrative examples are in feet. TRRP Change 26, effective 1 October 2016, defines exterior depth from water marks relative to the lowest adjacent grade and interior depth relative to the lowest floor — the top of the floor in non-V zones and the bottom of the floor beam in V zones — and codes the “has not reached the main building” case as **+999** on both. Bulletin W-16022 required insurers to report exterior and interior depth beginning **1 October 2016** ([TRRP Plan](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/2025-07/jan_2015_consolidated_trrp.pdf); [TRRP Change 26](https://nfipservices.floodsmart.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/2025-07/trrp_change_26.pdf); [Bulletin W-16022](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/sites/default/files/bulletins/W-16022/w-16022.pdf)). For 2020–2025, a true null on exterior or interior depth is a required value that was not reported. **+99 and +999 are informative codes for water that did not reach the main building.** They are not the generic 99XX unknown, and they are not “depth unknown.” The v3 interior reference level and the Change 26 reference level are not reconciled in any source found here. The notebook shows both sentences. Negative depths stay negative: TRRP’s basement and V-zone examples are measurements below the reference level. Nofal and coauthors, using an earlier OpenFEMA claims file, also found negative depth on buildings without a basement, so the notebook adds a small table of negative `waterDepth` by basement type and does not delete the negatives ([Nofal et al., FLOODrisk 2020](https://doi.org/10.3311/floodrisk2020.11.14)). The unit conflict stays in the caption: v3 says inches and sometimes feet, the older TRRP examples are in feet, and Change 26’s newer fields are explicitly inches. The notebook does not multiply or divide by 12.

`nfip_claims/scripts/03_clean_impute.py` sets `waterDepth == 99` to NaN and sets exterior and interior depth at or above 999 to NaN, then builds the missingness flags from those recoded columns. That erases the only documented meaning of those numbers and mixes “did not reach the building” with “not reported.” The notebook counts 99, 999, and values at or above 999 on the raw columns, stores `water_did_not_reach_main` as its own flag, and stores `depth_unreported` only for true nulls. A local impression that claims at 99 “behave like unknown” can be a cross-tab beside the TRRP sentence. It does not replace the sentence, and it does not feed the imputer.

Elevation codes need the same split between the v3 sentence and the older manuals. v3 says `elevationDifference` of **9999.0** “indicates the field is not reported and/or used for this policy,” and it does not name 9990, −9999, or −9980 for base flood elevation, lowest adjacent grade, or lowest floor elevation ([NFIP Redacted Claims v3](https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3)). TRRP does: base flood elevation, lowest adjacent grade, and lowest floor elevation use **9999.0** for “not reported and/or used for this policy.” Change 26 allows PRP, Group Flood, MPPP, emergency, provisional, and tentative policies to report the default or blank, and allows 9999.0 on current-map base flood elevation for unnumbered A, AO, AR, A99, B, C, D, unnumbered V, and X. Zone labels in v3 match that split: A and V have no base flood elevation on the FIRM, AE and VE have one, and AO is sheet flow ([TRRP Plan](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/2025-07/jan_2015_consolidated_trrp.pdf); [TRRP Change 26](https://nfipservices.floodsmart.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/2025-07/trrp_change_26.pdf)). An archived 2005 data-element bulletin repeats 9999 for lowest floor elevation in the claims master file, which stores whole feet, and lists Mortgage Portfolio Protection Program system defaults of base flood elevation +9999, elevation difference +999, lowest floor elevation +9999, and original construction date `00000000` ([Bulletin W-05022](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/sites/default/files/bulletins/W-05022/w-05022.pdf)). Those MPPP defaults are legacy policy-reporting behavior, not a v3 claims code list. Older TRRP text also uses +999 as an elevation-difference default. The public v3 sentence that defines the field names 9999.0. A blank and a 9999.0 are the same idea stored two ways, and they are not the same storage choice, so the profile counts them separately before any flag collapses them. **9990, −9980, and 987 do not appear in the v3 sentences, the TRRP 9999.0 sentences, or the searched portions of W-05022.** They are extract extremes, counted in a second block. OIG’s invalid entries are the relevant background, not a license to call every huge number a sentinel. The cleaning script flags base flood elevation, lowest floor, and lowest adjacent grade at an absolute value of at least 9990, and elevation difference at an absolute value of at least 500. That threshold mixes the documented 9999.0 with undocumented 9990, with −9980, and with any real elevation difference of 500 feet or more. The notebook does not use 500, or 9990, as a sentinel cutoff.

The other large “holes” are readable from the same manuals, once FEMA’s definition is kept separate from what this extract happens to do.

`ficoNumber` is the event identifier FEMA assigned before June 2020. “EDN has replaced FICO,” and after June 2020 flood events are assigned an event designation number ([NFIP Redacted Claims v3](https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3)). The column is still published. It is not described as redacted or stored as a sentinel. An extract that is entirely null, including January–May 2020, is slightly stronger than the June 2020 sentence, and the notebook says so. The signal lives on `eventDesignationNumber` and `floodEvent`. A “FICO missing” indicator would be a constant. `floodCharacteristicsIndicator` is still defined, with codes for velocity, ponding, wave action, mudflow, and erosion. TRRP marks Flood Characteristics optional, sourced from the adjuster’s report. FEMA does not say the column was retired, and the FAQ’s low-confidence omission sentence cannot be pinned to a column that is still on the schema. `floodWaterDuration` is hours of water in the building, also optional, with no blank rule and no sentinel. A fully empty optional column is consistent with lost confidence. That is an inference, and the notebook labels it “unpopulated in this extract.”

Recovery amounts and `mostRecentRecoveryDate` are written when a later salvage, subrogation, or overpayment recovery exists. TRRP’s salvage field is a positive amount when the insurer later sells damaged property and can trace it to the claim. Salvage handled by leaving the property with the insured and reducing the payment “is not reported in this field.” Payment-recovery dates are required on the recovery transaction, not on every claim ([TRRP Plan](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/2025-07/jan_2015_consolidated_trrp.pdf)). Emptiness is the ordinary state of a claim with no booked recovery. FEMA does not define that blank as zero dollars. The notebook keeps `recovery_recorded` and uses the dollars only inside the recorded slice. The project dictionary shows these fields empty together on nearly every row; the notebook reprints the rate.

`elevationCertificateIndicator` is the high-missing field whose blank FEMA defines directly: an empty value “indicates that this information was not reported; it does not necessarily mean that these properties do not have an Elevation Certificate, just that one was not reported at the time the policy was written.” Filled codes distinguish no certificate, a certificate with or without a base flood elevation, and foundation diagrams ([NFIP Redacted Claims v3](https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3)). Under Risk Rating 2.0 an elevation certificate is no longer required to purchase coverage, because FEMA uses its own elevation data; a policyholder may still provide one for a potentially more favorable premium, and certificates remain in use for floodplain-management requirements. Phased implementation ran from **1 October 2021** through **1 April 2023** ([Risk Rating 2.0 FAQ](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/2025-07/fema-nfip-risk-rating-2.0-FAQs.pdf); [Risk Rating 2.0 renewal guide](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/2025-07/fema-Risk-Rating-2.0-Renewal-Guide.pdf)). The cleaning script’s `has_elevation_cert` flag is 1 when the indicator is non-null. That behavior is “a code was reported,” which includes the codes that mean no certificate. The name reads as “has a certificate.” The notebook renames it `elevation_cert_code_reported`.

`obstructionType` “describes the type of obstruction that may be present under an elevated building.” TRRP allows a blank obstruction type on post-FIRM non-elevated buildings. Code **60** may be used when insufficient information exists to pick a more specific type, in the crawlspace case the note describes, and a later note in the same plan calls type 60 a default for unknown ([TRRP Plan](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/2025-07/jan_2015_consolidated_trrp.pdf); [NFIP Redacted Claims v3](https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3)). Code 60 stays its own level. It is not a modal enclosure, and it is not the same bin as a blank. A blank on a non-elevated building is not applicable. A blank on an elevated building is undefined by FEMA; an `unknown` level there is a modeling judgment.

Non-payment reason codes exist to explain a close without payment. TRRP’s element “indicates the reason a building claim was closed without payment,” the reporting requirement is required, and a closed-without-payment case is counted as a case and not as a claim ([TRRP Plan](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/2025-07/jan_2015_consolidated_trrp.pdf)). Absence on a paid coverage is the expected state. Absence on a coverage whose net payment is an observed zero is the remainder that was required and is still empty. The notebook plots the codes with missing as its own level and does not mode-impute code 01.

Contents location, the contents deductible, and the three contents money fields have no “no contents policy” code. No-contents is the explicit zero on `totalContentsInsuranceCoverage`. Deductible code 0 is $500. Basement code **0 already means none**, so a blank basement type is not “none.” The project dictionary records one shared missing rate on contents damage, contents property value, and contents replacement cost. That is a single reporting switch. FEMA never says the blanks equal the coverage zero. The notebook cross-tabulates before it calls a row structural: coverage equal to zero and the money fields empty is structural; coverage above zero and the money fields empty is item nonresponse. Building damage of 0 is a reported zero. A null building-damage amount is an adjuster figure that was not recorded. Cause-of-damage codes B, C, and D are expedited handling without a site inspection, a follow-up inspection, and remote adjustment ([NFIP Redacted Claims v3](https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3)). They are the covariates that make a missing depth or damage amount intelligible. FEMA does not say those codes cause the null. The dictionary also says more than one cause code can be selected, while the published field is a single code. The notebook reports whether stored values are single codes or concatenations. Code 0 is “other,” not missing.

`floodEvent` and `eventDesignationNumber` blanks are the no-designation state the FAQ describes: if no event designation was assigned, the claim is still found by state and a date-of-loss range ([FAQs About NFIP Data](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/flood-maps-and-data/faqs-about-nfip-data)). They are not “cause of damage unknown.” The project dictionary’s empty rates for the name and the designation differ by enough that one cannot be a pure copy of the other. The notebook counts the overlap. The project drop of the designation as “redundant with `floodEvent`” does not survive that gap. Both stay in EDA. A binary designated-event flag is the later feature. The name is a group key. Neither code is imputed.

Paid-amount nulls are not defined as zero. The dictionary says a negative amount can appear when a check is reissued, and it defines net payment as the gross amount minus recoveries. It does not say a null gross amount is zero. TRRP still has a pending status with a reserve and no payment, or a claim part paid and part on reserve ([TRRP Plan](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/2025-07/jan_2015_consolidated_trrp.pdf)). The project dictionary’s finding that net payment is zero on the null gross-payment rows is a cross-check in this extract, and the same missing share appears on `mostRecentPaymentDate`. The notebook prints that cross-tab and, where the identity holds, stores the dollars as zero beside a label “no payment recorded in this extract.” Where it fails on a slice, those rows stay missing. The label stays local. A “payment was null” feature next to a target just filled with zero is the target again.

`originalConstructionDate` in v3 is only “the original date of the construction of the building.” TRRP is specific: permit date, construction date, or, for a post-FIRM substantial improvement, the improvement date; if the date is before 1900, “report this date as **January 1, 1900**” ([TRRP Change 23.1](https://nfipservices.floodsmart.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/2025-07/trrp_changes_23.1.pdf)). That is the only January 1 placeholder the plan states. The MPPP construction-date default is `00000000`, a different code, which the notebook counts separately from a parsed 1900-01-01, because the two codes are not the same rule. The project dictionary calls a large January 1 share “placeholders.” The cleaning script already keeps those dates, flags `construction_date_jan1`, and computes age from them, which is the right treatment for every January 1 except 1900. It still computes age from **1900-01-01**, which treats “older than 1900 by an unknown amount” as a building built in 1900. The notebook sets that one date to missing, sets `construction_pre_1900`, and does not subtract 1900 from the loss year. Other January 1 dates keep the year. The day flag says the day is untrustworthy. Age error from a wrong day is at most about one year. Dates after the loss, and years before 1850 that are not the 1900 code, become missing. The 1850 cutoff is a local judgment; FEMA’s written sentinel cutoff is 1900. A surviving 1850–1899 date is an edit failure, because pre-1900 dates were supposed to be reported as 1900-01-01. Age from the dates that remain is a rating age: TRRP can store an improvement date or, for a manufactured home, a park or placement date. `originalNBDate` is the original flood-policy date. No TRRP rule found here says January 1 policy dates are placeholders. Dates after the loss, or before 1968, become missing. Coordinates that are present are coarsened observations. The blanks are the ungeocoded remainder. The notebook reports missing percent, distinct count, and the share of rows that share a rounded point, with the six-mile warning in the same cell. One-decimal points are not flagged as missing. `reportedCity` is neither nonresponse nor a city level.

Little and Rubin apply only to the item-nonresponse pile. Missingness is MCAR when the probability of a missing value depends on neither the observed nor the unobserved data, MAR when it depends only on observed data, and MNAR when it depends on the unobserved values. MCAR and MAR are the ignorable mechanisms for likelihood inference when the missingness parameters are distinct from the data parameters; this file cannot show that distinctness, so the notebook does not use the word “ignorable” ([Fielding, Maclennan, Cook, and Ramsay](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2711047/)). Little’s 1988 test checks whether groups defined by the missingness pattern have the same means on the variables they all observe. A significant result rejects MCAR. It does not choose between MAR and MNAR. A non-significant result does not prove MCAR ([MCAR, MAR, and MNAR](https://casrai.org/guides/mcar-mar-and-mnar-diagnosing-your-missing-data)). MCAR is already implausible on substantive grounds for the high-missing columns, because the reporting rules name observed covariates: elevated building, flood zone, rate method, contents coverage, paid versus without-payment close, cause-of-damage inspection codes, and whether a catastrophe number was issued. The notebook says “MCAR rejected” only after a grouped comparison or Little’s test, and it tests each shared pattern once — one indicator for the elevation block, one for the contents block, one for recovery recorded, one for a designated event — so the same switch is not counted five times. A MAR working assumption is reasonable only with the conditioning fields named. MNAR stays open for a specific reason the notebook writes down: a certificate can still be ordered because Risk Rating 2.0 lets the owner submit one for a lower premium; an adjuster who records hours or velocity is more likely to do so when the water was unusual; depth can be missing because the water never left a mark; an ungeocoded address can be missing because the address itself is nonstandard. The wording that does not overclaim is: after structural blanks and sentinels are removed, missingness in the field is associated with the named observed fields, so MCAR is rejected; a MAR working assumption conditional on those fields is reasonable for description; MNAR remains possible for the one substantive reason; the observed data cannot separate those two. Michel-Kerjan and Kousky’s 2010 Florida study dropped incomplete rows and analyzed the remainder. Listwise deletion is valid for means and regression weights under MCAR, and it can severely bias them otherwise ([Come Rain or Shine, 2010](https://repec.udesa.edu.ar/pub/Finanzas/Journals/Journal%20of%20Risk%20&%20Insurance/2010/Vol.%2077%20Issue%202/50211367.pdf); [van Buuren, §1.3.1](https://stefvanbuuren.name/fimd/sec-simplesolutions.html)). The notebook does not copy that drop under a silent MCAR assumption. The crosses that decide the pile — obstruction by elevated-building indicator, contents blanks by contents coverage, elevation 9999.0 by rated zone and rate method, non-payment blanks by whether net payment is zero, event-name blanks by designation blanks — were not computed in the research notes. The reporting rules say what they should show. The notebook computes them before a row is called structural.

## The 60 percent rule drops fields the dictionary still defines

EDA shows every column in the project dictionary. A later feature matrix drops a column for a reason that can be stamped in the presence table, and the stamp says whether the reason is a FEMA definition or a modeling judgment. Leakage, in the data-mining sense, is the introduction of information about the target that should not legitimately be available to mine from, and a legitimate feature is observable before the prediction time of the target ([Kaufman, Rosset, Perlich, and Stitelman, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1145/2382577.2382579)). The dataset does not name that prediction time. The notebook tags columns from the verbs in their definitions and leaves the choice of prediction time visible. Net payment is gross payment minus recoveries. Non-payment reasons are why a claim was closed without payment. Recovery fields and `mostRecentRecoveryDate` are post-payment. `mostRecentPaymentDate` is the date a payment was issued. `replacementCostBasis` “indicates whether the building claim was settled” on replacement cost or actual cash value. Damage, property value, and water marks are adjuster figures. Replacement cost is “as reported by the insurer.” Cause codes B, C, and D are the expedited path ([NFIP Redacted Claims v3](https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3)). If the target is payment, the paid amounts are the target, and nets, recoveries, non-payment reasons, and payment dates are functions of that outcome. If the prediction time is notice of loss, adjuster damage, depth, cause, property value, and settlement basis are later information. That second split is a modeling judgment from the dictionary verbs. FEMA does not publish a first-notice field list. ydata-profiling surfaces constants and high cardinality and then leaves the drop to the domain; it does not set a 60 percent missingness rule ([ydata-profiling](https://github.com/data-centric-ai-community/fg-data-profiling)).

The project’s “drop if missingness exceeds 60 percent” rule is a student heuristic. It is stamped today on the elevation block, the elevation-certificate indicator, obstruction type, flood-water duration, and disaster-assistance coverage. The notebook removes that justification. Elevation numbers stay out of the feature matrix because FEMA says to use elevation for description and aggregation, and because 9999.0 means not reported or not used, not because a cutoff was crossed. The availability flag `elevation_reported` is eligible for a predictive model. It means a number was present after the documented sentinel was removed. It does not mean the floor is high. Duration stays out because filling it would invent an optional adjuster column that is empty on nearly every row, and a missingness indicator on that column would be almost constant. Disaster assistance stays out for the same variance reason: code **0 already means “Not Required,”** so a blank is not zero, and the project dictionary says almost all of the non-missing values are already 0. A model flag would be the indicator method with nothing left to predict. TRRP called the element required except for MPPP and group policies, and the dictionary does not explain a majority-blank column ([TRRP Plan](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/2025-07/jan_2015_consolidated_trrp.pdf)). The notebook tabulates 0, 1, 2, and 5 against missing and does not fill missing with 0. Obstruction is not dropped for the cutoff at all; its handling is the not-applicable / code 60 / unknown split above.

The same separation decides the rest of the ignore list. `asOfDate` and `reportedCity` are a file timestamp and a withheld city, constant in this extract. Drop them from features, with no flag. The missing rows are not documented as “no ICC eligibility,” so they are not filled with 30000. `ficoNumber` is dropped as the retired identifier. `floodCharacteristicsIndicator` is dropped as unpopulated here, with the sentence “FEMA still defines this field.” Community numbers, community name, and `id` are identifiers. The same device is acceptable when the blank means not applicable, and it is acceptable for pure prediction when future claims will be missing in the same pattern and the coefficient is not the object ([Harrell, Regression Modeling Strategies, ch. 3](https://hbiostat.org/rmsc/missing); [van Buuren, §1.3.7](https://stefvanbuuren.name/fimd/missing-data-pattern.html); [Donders et al., 2006](https://www.jclinepi.com/article/S0895-4356(06)00197-1/fulltext)). That is why the 20-to-50 percent categorical holes become an explicit level. Basement type, contents location, and building description have finite legal lists and no missing code. ## Median fills skew less, and still plants a spike

Once the table above has moved documented codes and structural zeros out of the hole, very little is left that a numeric imputer should touch. Floors, the small condominium-type remainder, the invalid ages and tenures, the ungeocoded coordinates, and negative report lags are the list. Everything else is a level, a flag, or a field the model should not see. If the later model can split on missing values, even that short list does not have to be filled for the model to run. Sentinel recodes and structural zeros still have to happen, because a tree that sees 9999 as an elevation, or 99 as a depth in inches, will split on the code as if it were a measurement. scikit-learn’s own imputation example notes that `HistGradientBoostingRegressor` can use missing values natively and is often preferable to a heavy imputation pipeline ([IterativeImputer variants](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/impute/plot_iterative_imputer_variants_comparison.html)).

The methods that are cheap on a few hundred thousand rows are the univariate fills, and each one leaves a signature the notebook should plot. Listwise deletion is unbiased for the mean, the regression weight, and the correlation under MCAR, and its standard errors are valid for the reduced sample and usually larger than a method that keeps incomplete rows. With many columns it throws away a large share of the file. It is not always worse than imputation; it is the wrong default here because MCAR is implausible ([van Buuren, §1.3.1 and Table 1.1](https://stefvanbuuren.name/fimd/sec-simplesolutions.html)). Pairwise deletion is consistent for means and covariances under MCAR and can produce a covariance matrix that is not positive definite, especially when variables are highly correlated. van Buuren does not recommend it outside low-correlation, roughly normal, MCAR problems. A claims file is mixed, zero-inflated, and skewed. If the notebook shows a correlation, it prints the pairwise complete count beside each pair.

Mean imputation replaces every hole with the observed mean. It is fast. It underestimates the variance, disturbs relationships, and biases the mean itself when the data are not MCAR. In van Buuren’s air-quality illustration, imputing the mean of ozone created a visible spike, cut the standard deviation from **33 to 28.7**, and dropped the correlation with solar radiation from **0.35 to 0.30**. The advice is to use it only when a handful of values are missing ([van Buuren, §1.3.3](https://stefvanbuuren.name/fimd/sec-simplesolutions.html)). `SimpleImputer(strategy="mean")` does this per column and accepts numeric data only; mean is the library default ([SimpleImputer](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.impute.SimpleImputer.html)). Median imputation has the same spike-and-shrunk-variance geometry. The spike sits at a more typical dollar when the column is right-skewed, which is why the median is the better univariate default for a claim size. No source in this pass gave a theorem that median imputation is unbiased. The operation is just the column median. Zero inflation changes the choice in a specific way: if more than half of the observed values are zero, the median is zero, and median imputation converts every hole into “no payment.” The notebook prints the share of exact zeros, on observed rows only, before it chooses the median. Insurance total loss is modeled as a point mass at zero plus a skewed positive severity distribution. Severity itself is described as right-skewed and often heavy-tailed, and a single normal mean is a poor summary ([Klein, Lang, and others](https://cdn.uclouvain.be/public/Exports%20reddot/stat/documents/DP2013_45_ALL.pdf); [Počuča et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167668720300846)). The mean remains the right functional when the question is total dollars, and the notebook labels it as a total. A coverage cap pulls the mean toward the legal maximum and usually leaves the median alone. Imputed dollars above the cap that applies to that policy are not plausible payments.

Mode imputation is the categorical analogue of the mean. It makes the most common code more common and the rare codes rarer. If several values tie, `most_frequent` returns only the smallest, which can prefer a low code for no substantive reason ([SimpleImputer](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.impute.SimpleImputer.html)). At the project’s basement, contents-location, CRS, and building-description missingness, a mode fill would assign the majority code to a large block of policies, and the codebook already uses that majority code for a real structural class. An explicit missing level is the EDA and prediction device when the fact of missingness is known at prediction time. For a linear model the notebook dummy-encodes `unknown` and does not interpret that coefficient as the effect of a basement or a CRS discount. For a tree, the extra level is enough and a second indicator is redundant. A constant 0 is appropriate only when missing means none. `SimpleImputer(strategy="constant")` with a numeric `fill_value` of None becomes 0, and `keep_empty_features=True` fills an all-missing column with 0. That setting would turn `ficoNumber` and `floodCharacteristicsIndicator` into a column of zeros. The default, which drops an all-missing column at transform, is safer and still has to be stated, because a dropped column changes the matrix shape ([SimpleImputer](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.impute.SimpleImputer.html)).

Group-wise medians help when a coarse group has a different typical amount and missingness is roughly random inside the group. No scikit-learn imputer in the 1.9.1 guide computes a median inside groups; `SimpleImputer` statistics are column-wide ([sklearn imputation guide](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/impute.html)). The surveyed analogue is the hot-deck adjustment cell: donors inside classes that are homogeneous in both the outcome and the response probability reduce nonresponse bias, and over-stratifying adds noise ([Andridge and Little](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3130338/)). The notebook prints cell counts beside the within-group statistic and counts how often a thin cell falls back to a coarser median. Stratifying on the payment, or on anything built from the payment, copies the target into the feature.

Deterministic regression imputation writes the fitted prediction into the hole. The filled values vary less than real data, they lie on a curve, and correlations are biased upward. In the same air-quality example the ozone correlation rose from 0.35 to about **0.39**. Means are unbiased under MCAR, and regression weights of the imputation model are unbiased under MAR if the factors that drive missingness are in the model. van Buuren calls this, including machine-learning versions that still write a single prediction, a way to manufacture relationships that are too strong ([van Buuren, §1.3.4](https://stefvanbuuren.name/fimd/sec-simplesolutions.html)). Stochastic regression adds a residual so the cloud has scatter. It can preserve weights and correlations when the model is right, and it fails in ordinary ways on non-negative, heteroscedastic dollars: the air-quality draw produced a negative imputed ozone, and a linear model can predict negatives before noise is added. The standard error from one stochastic fill is still too small ([van Buuren, §1.3.5 and Table 1.1](https://stefvanbuuren.name/fimd/sec-simplesolutions.html)). `IterativeImputer` will leave predictions outside any business range unless `min_value` and `max_value` are set; both default to infinity ([IterativeImputer](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.impute.IterativeImputer.html)).

Predictive mean matching and hot deck are the methods that automatically respect a coverage cap and a point mass at zero, because they copy an observed dollar. Predictive mean matching finds a small pool of observed cases whose predictions are close — van Buuren says typically 3, 5, or 10, and the mice default he states is 5 — and draws one of those values. It stays inside the observed range, handles discrete targets, and is fairly robust to whether the target is imputed on the log scale or the raw scale. It cannot extrapolate, and it cannot interpolate through a sparse gap. Kleinke found that it generally worked unless the data were highly skewed and more than about **20 to 30 percent** had to be imputed, which is exactly the shape of a badly missing claim amount ([van Buuren, §3.4](https://stefvanbuuren.name/fimd/sec-pmm.html); [Kleinke, 2017](https://doi.org/10.3102/1076998616687084)). Hot deck has the same virtue and the same limit: the donor pool is an assumption, adjustment cells empty out as covariates are added, and one hot-deck file has no consensus interval ([Andridge and Little](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3130338/)). scikit-learn 1.9.1’s imputation guide documents `SimpleImputer`, `IterativeImputer`, `KNNImputer`, and `MissingIndicator`. It does not document predictive mean matching. A student who implements it should run it on a sample and show that every imputed value is one of the observed values, plus how often the most-used donor was reused.

`KNNImputer` fills each missing feature with the mean of the nearest training rows, default five, under a Euclidean distance that ignores coordinates missing on either row. Averaging neighbors invents numeric values that never occur in the data, including dollars no policy ever paid. If fewer than the requested neighbors exist, it falls back to the training mean. Because it stores the training matrix, it is not a full-file method on a laptop until a sample has been timed. No timing at NFIP row counts was found ([KNNImputer](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.impute.KNNImputer.html)). Iterative imputation, or MICE, specifies a univariate model for each incomplete column and cycles. `IterativeImputer` is still experimental: the estimator and the stopping rule can change without a deprecation cycle, the default estimator is `BayesianRidge`, the default initial fill is the mean, and early stopping applies only when `sample_posterior` is false. A random-forest backend writes the average of the trees, which is deterministic regression imputation unless a residual is added, and `sample_posterior=True` requires an estimator whose `predict` accepts `return_std`. Bayesian ridge is the multivariate option to try first, on a column subset or a row sample. A forest backend is the one to treat as too heavy for the full extract. missForest, in the 2012 paper and in R package 1.6.1, fits a forest to the observed part of each variable and predicts the rest, and it handles mixed types without a parametric distribution. The paper’s out-of-bag error tracked imputation error and tended to underestimate it as the missing fraction grew. The released object is one completed matrix plus that error, not a set of imputations pooled with Rubin’s rules. The phrase “multiple imputation” in the paper is about averaging trees, which is a different object from Rubin’s between-imputation variance ([Stekhoven and Bühlmann, 2012](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/28/1/112/219101); [missForest 1.6.1](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/missForest/refman/missForest.html)). No maintained Python package of that name was verified. The student’s installed library versions were not checked; the API details above are from the scikit-learn 1.9.1 and seaborn 0.13.2 pages read for this pass.

Single imputation cannot represent uncertainty about which value was filled. Standard errors from a completed table are too small even when the imputation model is correct, because the formulas treat the fill as observed ([Little and Rubin, single-imputation chapter](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781119482260.ch4); [Rubin, multiple imputation](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/12-001-x/1986001/article/14439-eng.pdf)). An NCES appendix, citing that literature, gives about **2 to 10 percent** underestimation of the variance of a mean or total at 5 percent nonresponse, and as much as **10 to 50 percent** at 30 percent nonresponse, varying by method. The same appendix quotes Little and Rubin that nonresponse bias often matters more than variance bias, and that a valid variance around a badly biased estimate can be worse than no variance ([NCES Statistical Standards, Appendix B](https://www.nces.ed.gov/statprog/2002/appendixb4.asp)). scikit-learn returns one array. `sample_posterior=True` is the switch for multiple imputations, which means the student redraws and pools outside the library. For a course model whose graded product is a predictive score, one imputation inside a pipeline is the practical artifact, and the write-up says the scores ignore imputation uncertainty. For an interval around a mean or a coefficient, multiple imputation is the method, and a cheap sensitivity check is two or three stochastic fills and the range of the resulting mean.

`log1p` is the scale for seeing the positive tail of a non-negative amount, because `log1p(0)` is defined and the tail spreads out. It does not remove the pile of zeros: they remain a spike at 0 on the transformed axis. It is undefined for the negative reissues, which are counted and set aside before the transform. Imputing a mean on the `log1p` scale and returning with `expm1` does not reproduce the arithmetic mean of dollars. The median does commute with a monotone transform, so a median on the raw scale and a median on `log1p` that is then back-transformed are the same dollar median. seaborn’s `histplot(..., log_scale=True)` log-transforms the data and bins in log space. That is not `log1p`, and it drops or breaks zeros ([seaborn.histplot](https://seaborn.pydata.org/generated/seaborn.histplot.html)). The notebook builds an explicit `log1p` column of the non-negative amounts and plots it on a linear axis.

The filled value is a statistic of the rows used to fit the imputer. If those rows include the test set, or later years in a time split, the training features contain information from the data used to judge the model. scikit-learn defines that as leakage and names `SimpleImputer` next to `StandardScaler` as a transformer that must be learned on the training subset only ([scikit-learn pitfalls, §12.2](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/common_pitfalls.html)). A pandas fill on the whole frame, followed by a split, cannot be refit inside each cross-validation fold. A transformer inside a `Pipeline` can ([Data School, leakage](https://www.dataschool.io/machine-learning-data-leakage/)). The year split is the one that matches a claims file: a later hurricane season should not help choose the median that fills an earlier season if the score is supposed to be a forecast. Group-wise medians leak the same way if the cell statistic uses all years. k-nearest neighbors leaks more richly, because a fit on all rows lets test claims, including claims from the same event, donate to each other. Iterative imputation leaks through every column used as a predictor. `MissingIndicator` emits a column only for features that were missing at fit time, and the default is to raise if a later year is missing a column the training years always observed ([MissingIndicator](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.impute.MissingIndicator.html)). It is legitimate for EDA to compute a median on the full extract in order to see the spike. That chart answers what the fill does to the distribution. It does not answer what score a model will get. The notebook says that in a sentence next to the figure, not only in a code comment.

## Twelve displays have to keep zeros, caps, and sentinels

The drawing tools are pandas for the counts, seaborn or matplotlib for distributions and category bars, and missingno for the missingness bar, matrix, and nullity heatmap. scikit-learn’s imputers belong in a later pipeline. They are not plotting tools. missingno’s matrix is comfortable up to about 50 labeled variables, its README shows a 250-row sample, and its heatmap is the Pearson correlation of the boolean missingness indicators after columns that are always full or always empty have been dropped. A correlation near 1 means the fields are missing together. A correlation near −1 means one is present when the other is absent. The heatmap is not a correlation of the dollars, and it has no particular support for a file of this size ([missingno](https://github.com/residentmario/missingno)). The notebook draws the matrix on a sample of a few hundred to a few thousand rows, stratified by year so that early years are not omitted. The README’s 250-row figure is a display sample, not a required statistical sample size. Running missingno after a fill is useless: the missingness is gone.

Three mistakes change the story of this file. Annotating only the mean on a payment histogram overstates the typical claim and hides how many claims are zero or small. A histogram drawn after imputation, with filled rows painted the same color as observed rows, hides the spike inside a bin and makes the variance look like a property of the claims. A scatter or a kernel density of one-decimal coordinates produces hot spots at grid intersections; those hot spots are the rounding, and a smaller marker does not make them buildings. A state choropleth of the mean payment is dominated by the same skew and caps as the histogram. Counts, totals, or medians answer cleaner geographic questions, and the state field is the field FEMA says to use when a truncated point falls in the wrong state ([FAQs About NFIP Data](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/flood-maps-and-data/faqs-about-nfip-data)).

The same sources imply four quieter mistakes. A boxplot of raw payments with the default 1.5-interquartile-range fence will flag a large share of ordinary large claims, because the fence assumes a batch without this tail ([seaborn.boxplot](https://seaborn.pydata.org/generated/seaborn.boxplot.html); [Velleman and Hoaglin, ch. 1](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/21d51b51-a50f-4418-b1d7-cd6e49907ef9/content)). A kernel density on a zero-inflated amount invents a smooth curve over a point mass. A correlation heatmap of dollar columns after mean imputation shows weaker relationships than the observed rows, and after regression imputation shows stronger ones. A category bar that drops missing values understates the incomplete level and inflates the shares of the levels that remain. Tukey’s stem-and-leaf keeps individual values and is not usable at this row count. The histogram and the boxplot are the practical descendants, and the boxplot is read on the `log1p` scale or on the positive subset when the raw scale crushes the box against the axis.

The cross-tabs from the missingness section sit with these figures, not in an appendix the model never sees: pre-FIRM against post-FIRM, rated zone against current zone, construction-date counts for 1900-01-01 against other January 1 dates against every other day, negative depth against basement type, and the payment-null identity. Each caption says which sentence is FEMA’s and which number was computed from this extract.

## Conclusion

The notebook is the audit of the cleaning script, computed from the raw extract, with the cleaned parquet used only as a diff. Three choices in `03_clean_impute.py` already outrun `decisions.py`: CRS is median-imputed after the decision comment specified an unknown category, settlement basis is mode-filled, and coordinates are filled by a county code that repeats across states. Two more choices outrun TRRP: water-depth 99 and 999 become ordinary missingness, and buildings coded 1900-01-01 receive an age measured from 1900. An EDA that starts from the cleaned file will rediscover a smoother extract than FEMA published and will then impute a hole the manuals had already filled with a meaning.

The v3 field table is the authority for what this file publishes, and it is incomplete relative to the reporting manuals the FAQ still points at. A notebook that cites only the OpenFEMA page will miss the water-depth codes, the elevation 9999.0 rule on the fields v3 forgets to mention, and the single official January 1 date. Citing the manuals without saying they are manuals will overstate what the public dictionary promises. The practical consequence for a data-mining course is that “which fields have to be imputed” is the wrong question to ask first. After documented codes and structural zeros are stored as themselves, numeric imputation is optional whenever the model can split on missingness, and it is harmful wherever it overwrites a code FEMA already defined. Multiple imputation earns its cost when the graded product is an interval. A single pipeline fill is enough for a predictive score, provided the write-up says the score treats guesses as observations. The file’s own lag does the rest: 2025, and late 2024, are still moving, and no chart of a filled column should be asked to look like a finished year.
